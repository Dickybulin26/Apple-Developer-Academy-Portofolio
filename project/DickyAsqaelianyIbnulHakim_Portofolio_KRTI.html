NATIONAL DRONE COMPETITION

Kontes Robot Terbang Indonesia (KRTI) 2026 — Indonesia's national flying-robot competition hosted by Institut Teknologi Kalimantan, where our Universitas Gunadarma team designed, built, and field-tested a fixed-wing UAV and brought home the Best Prototype award.

KRTI is a national-level competition where university teams across Indonesia compete with flying robots they design from scratch. Our entry was a fixed-wing UAV built over months of design iterations, bench tests, and field trials. I worked on the build and integration of the aircraft — from airframe assembly and avionics wiring to telemetry setup and pre-flight checks — culminating in the team being awarded Best Prototype at KRTI 2026.

Challenge

National competitions leave no room for guesswork — the UAV had to be reliable enough to survive repeated field trials, transportable between cities, and compliant with a strict rulebook on size, weight, and flight behavior. Weather at the field is unpredictable, and every failed launch costs time we don't have, so the aircraft needed to be right the first time it left the rail.

What I built

I took charge of the hands-on build and integration: assembling and balancing the airframe, wiring the avionics bay — flight controller, ESCs, servos, and battery — and configuring the telemetry link between the aircraft and our laptop ground station. On top of that, I ran pre-flight checklists and supported the team through every bench test and field trial leading up to the competition.

Impact

The aircraft performed consistently through the field rounds and the team brought home the Best Prototype award at KRTI 2026. Representing Universitas Gunadarma on a national stage proved that our team can deliver engineering work that stands up to scrutiny alongside universities from across Indonesia — and put a concrete, awarded result behind everything we had been practicing all along.

What I learned

Field engineering teaches discipline that desk work never can: how a single loose connector or badly balanced surface can end a flight, and why checklists and redundancy matter more than clever designs. I also learned to stay calm and methodical under competition pressure, and how a team that trusts each other's roles can turn months of messy iteration into a single, successful launch.
